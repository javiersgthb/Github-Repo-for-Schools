Amending the United States Constitution

The US constitution isn’t easily amended, but it can be changed if needed. As you’ll see, the amendment process takes time. The Constitution has only been amended 27 times since it was adopted as the plan of government. If you take out the Bill of Rights (the first 10 amendments to the Constitution), that’s only 17 amendments in roughly 230 years. In some ways, this can be considered a testament to the enduring quality of the Constitution. In other ways, it’s an indicator of the difficulty of changing the Constitution.

There are multiple ways for the Constitution to be amended:

  1. 1.  An amendment may be proposed by Congress with a two-thirds majority vote in both houses.

               or

    2.  An amendment may be proposed by a Constitutional Convention called by Congress upon application of the state legislatures of two-thirds of the states (in other words, 34 of 50 states).

  2. An amendment is ratified by the state legislatures of, or conventions in, three-fourths of the States (in other words, 38 of 50 states).


Because of these requirements, you can see how changing the Constitution could be very difficult. In modern political times, the closely matched division of power between the two major parties almost guarantees there will be no amendments added any time soon. In a way, this can be a good thing because the majority of the nation needs to be supportive of an amendment before it can be added. The amendments that were added were put in the Constitution because of an apparent need, overwhelming political support, or because the change was so noncontroversial that few would disagree or wage the political fight to stop it.

What was the last amendment to the Constitution?

The 27th, an uncontentious amendment regarding Congressional pay, was originally proposed in 1789 but wasn’t ratified until 1992.
