Parts of the Constitution:  Articles IV–V

Article IV—Federalism/The States

Article IV of the Constitution defined the federal nature of the United States, allowing for a national (federal) government and many different state governments, each level with their own powers and responsibilities. This article laid out relations between the states and the states’ interactions with the federal government. Just like with the earlier articles, the definition of states’ rights or the extent of federal power wasn’t exactly spelled out. It would take years of court cases, amendments to the Constitution, and hard-fought political battles to reach the relationship between the states and the federal government understood today.

Article V—The Amendment Process

Article V outlined how changes,  known as amendments, could be made to the Constitution. You’ll learn about the specifics of the amendment process later in this module. The Founding Fathers knew that the Constitution would need to be changed from time to time, but they didn’t want changes to come too easily. In fact, many major changes to the way American government operates came with evolving interpretations of the Constitution rather than explicit amendments. Removing an amendment from the Constitution requires adding a new amendment voiding the earlier amendment.

How many times has an amendment been removed from the Constitution?

This has happened once so far in US history. The 18th Amendment put alcohol prohibition in place. The 21st Amendment repealed the 18th Amendment.
