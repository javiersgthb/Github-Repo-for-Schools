Trends in Political Identification

Using polls, election results, and studies on political socialization, people who study politics can make fairly accurate generalizations regarding what groups of people are likely to vote for what political parties.

There are many different factors that can help them analyze groups.

Select each item to learn more.

Income/Occupation

Voters in lower income brackets tend to vote Democratic, while those with higher incomes tend to vote Republican. Of course, there are outliers, but with higher income comes more of a tendency to vote Republican.

Education

College graduates tend to vote for Republicans in higher percentages than those whose highest educational level is a high school diploma. However, high school graduates vote Republican in higher percentages than those who have only gone through grade school. Traditionally, the Republican Party has had the advantage with Americans with four-year college degrees, while the Democratic Party has had the advantage with Americans with graduate degrees. In recent years, the Republican Party has lost most of its advantage with the four-year degree group.

Gender

There’s definitely a gender gap. Women typically prefer Democrats by a margin of 5 to 10 percent. Men give the Republican Party a similar edge. This divide has become much more noticeable with the Trump presidency. Men and women are likely to vote differently on issues such as abortion, healthcare, social welfare programs, and military involvements abroad.

Age

Younger voters (30 years of age and under) are more likely to vote Democratic. Older voters (50 years of age and older) are more likely to vote Republican. There’s a misconception that people tend to become more conservative as they get older. This isn’t necessarily true. As social norms change, an older person may have views that seem old fashioned or more “traditional.” This could be a result of a changing society rather than the person becoming more conservative.

Religion

Jews and Catholics tend to lean Democratic (with the 2004 elections being notable exceptions). Protestants tend to lean Republican. Evangelical Christians make up a huge base of support for the Republican Party. People who go to church at least once a week lean Republican. This is the key point—when it comes to Christianity and Judaism, the more religious a person is, the more likely he or she is to vote Republican. Issues affect that turnout of these voters. In the past, ballot measures regarding same-sex marriage or abortion have tended to increase religious voter turnout.

Ethnic Background

Black Americans vote overwhelmingly for the Democratic Party. Hispanic voters have tended to vote for the Democratic Party. Recent issues can affect this leaning, such as immigration reform and the Catholic religion of most Latinos. White males give the Republican Party a large advantage.

Geography

Urban areas tend to vote Democratic, and suburban/rural regions tend to vote Republican—although recent elections have shown the suburbs may be up for grabs. The northeastern/West Coast states trend Democratic, while the southern/midwestern/northern states trend Republican. The biggest factor at play here seems to be the rural/urban divide.

Family and Social Groups

Nine out of ten couples share the same partisan leanings. Two out of three voters have the same political leanings as their parents. Those who work together and share friendships also tend to vote very much alike. This is related to political socialization. People base their views in the culture in which they were brought up and live.

Remember that these are all generalizations. There are black Republican voters and Protestant Democratic voters. Despite the outliers, however, these trends are generally correct. You could reasonably guess how a person is likely to vote if you knew demographic information about him or her. Party identification is increasingly connected to where Americans live. In particular, there’s a stark rural/urban divide. Part of this can be attributed to the daily experience each group has with governmental services, and part of it can be attributed to political socialization.

On the surface, these trends appear to be troubling for the Republican Party, in particular. Women, younger people, and Hispanic Americans are trending toward the Democratic Party.

Why would this matter?

Women are more likely to vote than men, younger people represent the future of the electorate, and Hispanic Americans are the fastest-growing ethnic population in the United States. All of these signs point toward a difficult future in gaining votes for the Republican Party.

Despite all of the trends, experts know to be cautious in making ironclad predictions. As we’ve seen throughout history, groups of people can change their minds when it comes to political preferences. For now, the structure and federal nature of the United States government benefits the Republican Party. Since Republicans have the advantage in rural areas and each state gets two senators regardless of population, the Republican Party has a built-in advantage in the Senate for at least the near future.
