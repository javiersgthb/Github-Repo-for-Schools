Measuring Public Opinion Through Elections

The first method of measuring what the voting public thinks is by looking at election results. This seems so obvious that it often gets overlooked. Yet election results certainly give an idea of what voters are thinking. In fact, with modern registration and demographic data, it’s entirely possible for political consultants to predict how groups of people are likely to vote, at least based on geography.

For example, say a town of 1,000 people votes in one polling location. This is the only location where those people are allowed to vote. While American elections are completed by secret ballot (meaning citizens cast their votes in private), you can see who voted in a particular election in terms of what party they’re registered to support. You also can see the breakdown of votes within that polling place and how many of the 1,000 people voted. In addition, public access is available to the demographic data of those registered voters through the US census and other polling methods. You can use all of this data to make a decent prediction of how different groups of people are likely to vote.

There are, however, drawbacks to this method of determining public opinion. Since elections are usually a choice between the two major parties, many voters pick the more likely winner even if it isn’t the perfect choice for them. A vote for a candidate isn’t necessarily an indication of support for the entire platform of the candidate’s party.

Voting against a person or party is known as negative partisanship.


Political parties and candidates who win like to claim a popular mandate when winning an election, but be wary of these claims. A vote for a candidate or a party has a good chance of simply being a vote against the other candidate or party. Recent studies have shown that negative partisanship is becoming an increasing motivator in why people vote the way they do. It can be said safely that many Americans vote out of dislike for an opposing candidate or party rather than a true preference for the candidate they cast their vote for. This negative partisanship can be seen in modern political campaigns that “go negative”—trying to hurt a candidate’s reputation by encouraging dislike or disgust in voters.
