Key Points

  • Public opinion is measured to get an idea of what voters want to see out of their government—and to help political parties win elections.
  • Elections can be an indirect way of measuring public opinion, but this measurement is limited because it’s difficult to know exactly why people vote the way they do.
  • Women, people with higher educations, and people with higher income are more likely to vote; men and people with little education and low incomes are less likely to vote.
  • Sociological factors such as education, income, race, and religion can be used to predict a person’s voting behavior. Such measures aren’t perfect but can give a political scientist a general idea of how the average person in a specific group is likely to vote.
  • Scientific polling attempts to measure public opinion using representative samples of the overall population to be measured.
  • Geography is a known factor in political affiliation. Rural areas tend to vote Republican, and urban areas tend to vote Democratic.
  • Turnout in elections increases if the elections themselves are competitive.
