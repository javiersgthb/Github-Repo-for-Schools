Voter Participation

Since the early 1900s, voter participation in major elections has dropped. The turnout rate among eligible voters hit its lowest point in 1996 at 49 percent for the presidential and 45.8 percent for the congressional elections. However, it’s rebounded slightly in recent years to around 55 percent in presidential elections. Even lower percentages are seen in off-year elections—congressional elections held in the even-numbered years between presidential elections. Off-year election turnout has swung wildly in recent years. In 2014, the United States had the lowest off-year election turnout in decades. However, four years later, 2018 showed the highest off-year turnout in decades. Generally speaking, the farther down the ballot an office is, the fewer the votes that will be cast for it.

There are some studied generalizations to allow for comparison of people likely to vote versus people not likely to vote. For example, women are more likely to vote than men. Other statistical characteristics include:

Voters

  • Higher levels of income, education, and occupational status
  • Long-time residents of the community
  • Strong party identification and belief that voting is an important act
  • Likely to live in areas where laws and competition between parties all promote voter turnout

Nonvoters

  • Likely to be younger than 35
  • Unmarried
  • Unskilled
  • More nonvoters live in the South and rural areas than in urban or suburban areas.

An important factor in whether or not people vote is if an election seems competitive. Think about it—would you be interested in a sporting event in which you knew who was likely to win ahead of time? In areas where an election can go either way, people feel that their votes matter more and are more likely to vote. Unfortunately, due to gerrymandering, geographical political segregation, and efforts to make voting more difficult, many elected offices are noncompetitive, and the favored candidate is almost certain to win. In such areas, voter turnout tends to decrease. Also, in areas where laws make it difficult or inconvenient to vote, turnout logically declines.
