The History of Voting Rights

While the right to vote may be largely taken for granted today, generations of Americans have advocated, struggled, and fought to secure these rights for themselves and for future generations since, as you will learn, the United States was not founded on the principle of voting equality for all.

During the pre-colonial era and early nationhood, most states restricted voting to land-owning adult white males. The period between 1812 and 1860 saw states begin to remove property ownership from the requirements to participate in elections, allowing all free white males to vote. After the civil war was over in 1865 the right to vote was extended to Black males.

However, many southern states ignored this change in the constitution and erected barriers, making it hard for black men to vote. Many former Confederate states implemented illegal restrictive voting laws to suppress Black voting rights. These included literacy tests, where citizens had to prove their ability to read and write before voting. Additionally, poll taxes required payment in order to cast a ballot, disproportionately affecting impoverished Black citizens.

Women were granted the right to vote in 1920 with the passage of the Nineteenth Amendment to the Constitution. Before this historic change, women were excluded from participating in the democratic process, limiting their influence and representation. With the amendment, women gained a powerful voice and became an essential part of the electorate. Their expanded political participation allowed them to advocate for social reforms and policies that improved their lives and communities.

The amendment also contributed to the growth of the women’s rights movement. Women began breaking traditional gender roles, pursuing higher education, and entering the workforce in larger numbers. The fight for suffrage sparked broader discussions about gender equality and paved the way for future advancements in women’s rights.
