Competing Interests

American government can be seen as groups of competing interests trying to influence how government is run. To this end, interest groups are formed to steer government in ways that would benefit the members of those groups.

Business Groups

Business groups make up more than one-half of all lobbying groups in Washington, D.C. These groups concentrate their activities on business policies, often represented by such associations as the National Association of Manufacturers and the US Chamber of Commerce.

Labor Groups

Labor groups seek to promote policies that benefit union members in particular but also workers in general. These groups have been politically active for a long time. The largest labor group is the AFL-CIO.

Farm Groups

Farm groups represent various segments of the farm community. Examples include the American Farm Bureau Federation, which promotes agribusiness and large farms, and the National Farmers Union, which promotes the interests of smaller family farms.

Professional Groups

Professional groups, such as the American Medical Association, represent various professions and attempt to influence policy on their behalf.

Interest groups also may be single-issue-based, such as the National Rifle Association, which promotes gun owners’ rights, or the Sierra Club, which champions environmental causes. They also might have a philosophical basis, such as the Club for Growth or People for the American Way. All are groups of people who work together to get what they want out of government.

Interest groups sound much like political parties, don’t they? The main difference is that political parties want to get their members elected to office to achieve policy goals; interest groups mainly care about influencing who gets into office, as pushing specific policies is their ultimate focus. This can certainly lead interest groups to support one major party over the other if the views of a party and interest group are aligned.
