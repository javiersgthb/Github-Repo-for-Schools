The Two-Party System

Have you ever wondered why it seems to be only the Republicans and Democrats winning elections or even being talked about as political forces? There are parties in America other than the two major parties, but they rarely get consideration. A few reasons for this exist. One is simply due to circumstance—the United States started out with a two-party system and has kept the tradition. People tend not to question traditions once they become ingrained. Other reasons are a bit more concrete. The two major political parties have written election laws in such a way that the laws benefit established parties while hurting third parties.

The dominance of two major political parties in the US can be attributed to several factors. Firstly, the US operates on a system of winner-take-all elections, also known as a plurality voting system. In this system, the candidate with the most votes in a given area wins the entire electoral district or state, leaving little room for smaller parties to gain a foothold. As a result, third parties often struggle to win enough support to secure meaningful representation.

In contrast, other democracies often have more political parties due to proportional representation systems. These systems allocate seats in government based on the percentage of votes each party receives nationally or regionally. Smaller parties can secure representation even if they don’t win outright majorities, encouraging a diverse political landscape.

To understand this, it’s important to understand the difference between a single-member district system and a proportional representation system.

Single-Member District System Proportional Representation System

Definition

A form of representation in which only the candidate who gets the most votes in a district wins office

A form of representation in which seats in the legislature are allocated proportionally according to each political party’s share of the popular vote

Example

In the US, if a minor party receives 20 percent of the vote or less in each congressional district, it wouldn’t win any seats in Congress. Despite the fact that one in five voters voted for the party, the winning candidate in each district would be the major-party candidate with the larger proportion of the remaining 80 percent of the vote.

In European democracies, seats in the legislature are allocated according to a party's share of the popular vote; if a minor party wins 20 percent of the vote, it receives 20 percent of the legislative seats. America's single-member district system disadvantages minor parties and, therefore, promotes a two-party system.

The single-member district system in the US promotes the two-party system and discourages minor parties because it’s essentially a winner-takes-all contest.
