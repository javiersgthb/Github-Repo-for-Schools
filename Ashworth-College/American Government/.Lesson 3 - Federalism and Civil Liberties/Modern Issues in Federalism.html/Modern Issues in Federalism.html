Modern Issues in Federalism

In the early days of the United States, the states had quite a bit of their own power. Not only did people in America see themselves as citizens of their states first, but the organization of government was tilted toward the state level. Since communication and travel was more difficult, reacting to events and effectively governing required proximity.

Since the founding of the nation, changes to the Constitution and historical events have shifted power away from the states and toward the federal (national) level of government.

For example, the Fifteenth Amendment took the power to grant citizenship away from the states and gave it to the federal government after Southern states hesitated to give formerly enslaved people citizenship.

The efforts to recover from the Great Depression and fight World War II resulted in a massive expansion in the power of the federal government.

Today, the federal government uses intergovernmental revenue, money given from one level of government to another, to incentivize states to follow the lead of the national government.

Educational Funding Acts

The 2002 No Child Left Behind Act (NCLB) and its 2015 follow up, Every Student Succeeds Act (ESSA), required standardized testing of every student for states to access federal education funding. 

All states engage in standardized testing as a way to measure educational outcomes, and laws like NCLB and the ESSA tied outcomes to funding. Like any other policy, these laws had good and bad results. If the states wanted, they had the constitutional right to refuse to engage in the testing, but then wouldn’t qualify for federal funds. This way, the federal government is essentially paying states to follow federal policies in areas that state governments constitutionally control.
