The Process of Incorporation

The Bill of Rights was originally intended to limit the power of the national government, not the power of state governments. To be fair, the states had their own constitutions that closely mimicked the US Constitution. However, there were instances where protections given to people through the Bill of Rights weren’t in effect on a state level. This began to change with the Fourteenth Amendment.

The Fourteenth Amendment contains a due process clause:

No State shall deprive any person of life, liberty, or property, without due process of law


In other words, no state can deny any person any right considered to be essential to the American concept of liberty. Since its adoption in 1868, the Fourteenth Amendment has been used in court to argue that the states must also respect many of the freedoms in the Bill of Rights. The concept of applying the freedoms of the Bill of Rights to state governments is called the process of incorporation.

All of the Bill of Rights except for the Third, Fifth (grand jury), and Seventh (trial by jury in civil cases) have been nationally incorporated through Supreme Court decisions. This is an ongoing process which may see more changes in the future—consider that the Second Amendment wasn’t incorporated until a Supreme Court decision in 2010.
