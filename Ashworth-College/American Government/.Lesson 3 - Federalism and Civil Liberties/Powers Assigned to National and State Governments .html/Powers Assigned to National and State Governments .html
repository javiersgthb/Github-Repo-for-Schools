Powers Assigned to National and State Governments

The American political system is balanced on the division of authority between a national government and the state governments. Before the Constitution, the Articles of Confederation described the first US government as basically a union of the states. The Constitution outlined powers given to the national (federal) level of government as well as powers given to the state level of government. It’s this division of power that makes the American government unique in its own way, which results in certain advantages and disadvantages.

Powers Given to the National Government

Declare War and Oversee Military

The Founding Fathers saw the danger in relying on each state to run its own military. What if different states decided to start wars on their own? The national government was given control of declaring war and a national military structure.

Make Treaties and Handle Foreign Policy

There was danger in the possibility of allowing each state to enter into negotiations with other nations. Under the Constitution, the United States would interact with foreign powers as a unified nation.

Issue Money

Under the Articles of Confederation, each state created and circulated its own currency. This made commerce between states difficult. With the new form of government, the national government issued all currency.

Regulate Trade

Without a higher authority to make decisions, commerce could grind to a halt if there were an economic disagreement between states. The Constitution gives the national government the power to set the rules for interstate commerce and regulate trade with foreign nations.

Powers Given to the State Governments

Provide Public Schooling

The establishment and management of public education is left up to the states. In fact, education makes up the single largest slice of state budgets.

Establish Local Governments

The Constitution only mentions state and national levels of government, not local. All counties, cities, townships, and parishes exist and have power because they’re recognized by their respective state governments. States have the power to split up, combine, or dissolve local governments. If there’s a conflict between state and local law, the state law wins out.

Regulate State Trade

Within state borders, each state has its own set of taxation and regulation of business and labor laws.

Conduct Elections

Each state has its own system for voting. Some states allow voting by mail and some only allow voting in person on Election Day. The states also differ in how they determine voter qualifications.

Provide Safety and Health Services

States oversee many programs for citizens’ health and welfare. Police and emergency services would fall under this category.
