The History of Democratic Government

The democratic form of government began in Greece. Between 750 and 550 BCE, Greece, a loose collection of city-states that operated as small independent nations, began to develop ideas about self-governance. In particular, the city-state of Athens began to be ruled by a grand council with the idea of embracing the concept of government by the people.

Athens was the first to develop and implement a large-scale democratic style of government. It was a direct democracy—people voted directly for their rulers and laws. People who were citizens were allowed to vote and participate in government. However, Athenian democracy was far from perfect. Only men who owned large amounts of property were allowed to be citizens. Only citizens determined how the government was run. Women, enslaved people, and small farmers or business owners had no voice in the governance of Athens.

Between 750 and 350 BCE, Romans developed the republic idea of democratic governance; at the same time, the Greek Athenians embraced their city-state style of direct democracy. In a republic, people voted for representatives to the Senate rather than everyone sharing in governance and every vote. These elected senators then participated in the process of making and passing laws.

Initially, only patricians (the upper class) could participate in government, but eventually, plebeian (lower class) individuals were also allowed to participate. The senators handled day-to-day governmental issues on behalf of the people. Around 450 BCE, Romans also began to write down and codify their laws to make it easier for the government and to prevent senators from abusing their power.

Democracy expanded beyond Greece and Rome over time. Aspects of it reached England by the 1100s, when King Henry II ruled as an absolute monarch. However, he created a unified court system and established English common law traditions around this time. After making this change, people could rely on precedent and history supported by courts to guide them in creating contracts and conducting business dealings.

By 1215, the barons (people who owned significant land) joined together and made then-King John sign the Magna Carta, which spelled out the rights of nobles and barons and restricted the powers of the king. It guaranteed a right to trial when charged with crimes and other manners. It also created a parliament to serve as a legislative body where the king, as the executive, had to share some power with the legislators.
