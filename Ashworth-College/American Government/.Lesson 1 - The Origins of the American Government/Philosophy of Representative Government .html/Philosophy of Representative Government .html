Philosophy of Representative Government

The people who would eventually create the framework for American government didn’t come up with their ideas all on their own. They were influenced by Enlightenment thinkers who came before them and wrote about the nature of human society. Both the Enlightenment thinkers and the founders of the United States came of age in a time when government didn’t reflect the will of the people ruled by their governments, which led them to seek the construction of something new.

Important political thinkers at the time of the Enlightenment included English philosophers Thomas Hobbes and John Locke as well as French philosopher and judge Charles Montesquieu.

Thomas Hobbes

Written in the post-English Civil War, Thomas Hobbes’ Leviathan (1651) assesses human character and illustrates problems with democracy. It proposed the idea of a social contract, which celebrates the rights and privileges of the individual without disparaging the concept of the King. According to the social contract theory, people are born with freedom but give up some of that freedom to live in a safe and orderly society.

Hobbes believed that people not served by their government (the king) had a responsibility to revolt. This belief wasn’t against government in general; rather, it specifically didn’t support unjust rule. Hobbes believed that humans naturally lived in a state of competition and strife, and without some form of government, life would be difficult.

John Locke

One of the most critical aspects of John Locke's philosophy is the idea of natural law and rights. Like Hobbes, Locke believed that all people are born with unassailable rights and freedoms, and that some of those freedoms are given up when a government is formed. Unlike Hobbes, Locke believed that all people have a duty to respect others’ rights. Locke’s ideas would help form the idea of rights and the responsibilities instrumental in constructing American government.

Charles Montesquieu

Montesquieu believed in individual and natural rights, but also introduced an idea that would be central to the future United States—separate and co-equal branches of government. The separate branches of American government enshrined in the Constitution of the United States were heavily based in Montesquieu’s work. The point of the separate branches was to ensure that corruption wouldn’t result from one person or group of people holding all of the power in a government.
