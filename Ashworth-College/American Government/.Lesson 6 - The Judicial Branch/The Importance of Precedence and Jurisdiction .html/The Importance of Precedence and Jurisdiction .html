The Importance of Precedence and Jurisdiction

Precedence

Legal principle developed through earlier court decisions, stating that a court's decision on a case should be consistent with previous rulings

Jurisdiction

A court’s jurisdiction is the authority to hear a case in that court.

The principle of precedent is an important factor in court rulings. Notice the root word precede, which means “to come before.” A precedent is something that occurred in the past that provides guidance on what to do in new situations. Judges in the United States tend to follow precedence. In many cases, this starts at a local level and then works its way up. In other words, most disputes start on a local level, and even those local judges tend to follow precedence. In the vast majority of crimes or disputes, local courts first handle these issues and create or follow precedence. Because local courts (subject to state law) tend to see cases first, there can be a large variety of precedents set. For example, if a law regarding a criminal act was interpreted one way in one county and then another way in a neighboring county, you could have two conflicting precedents in two counties in the same state.

How would these differences be reconciled?

A higher court would have to weigh in on that exact issue and put forth a precedent to be followed by lower courts. In the case of counties disagreeing on precedence, that state’s Supreme Court could issue a decision, setting the precedent for the whole state.

This principle leads to different precedents among the states themselves. For a wider precedent to be set, a case would have to go into the federal system. As you can see, different precedents are possible until a higher court makes a decision. The higher the court, the wider the precedent applies in terms of geography. The Supreme Court of the United States sets the precedents for the entire nation. Remember that while precedents tend to be followed, they can be overturned. In recent years, the Supreme Court has overturned precedents on issues such as campaign contributions and civil rights.

Jurisdiction is the legal power to make a binding legal decision. As you just learned, state courts have jurisdiction over matters involving state laws, and federal courts have jurisdiction over cases involving the Constitution or disputes between states, among others. The type of case and the parties involved determine which court has jurisdiction.

Original Jurisdiction

A court’s authority to be the first court to hear the facts of a case and make a decision based on those facts. When a court case begins, it goes to the court with original jurisdiction over that type of case. This is sometimes disputed by different courts who may have a similar claim to hear a legal case for the first time, such as when both a state and federal court insist that they have the original jurisdiction over a case.

Appellate Jurisdiction

A court’s authority to review cases that have already been tried in lower courts and are appealed to higher courts by the losing party. The appellate court can either change or uphold the ruling made by the court that came before them. Additionally, an appellate court may choose to not hear the case at all, in which case the original ruling stands.

Tribal Courts

In addition to federal and state courts, about 400 courts called tribal courts exist throughout the United States, such as that seen in the image. These courts have original jurisdiction in cases related to the laws of Native American nations. Native American nations manage these courts separately from the federal and state court systems. Some nations’ courts function similarly to federal and state courts, while others use a nation’s traditional judicial models, such as a council of elders who make decisions in cases. Much like any other federal court decisions in a tribal court may be appealed to a higher federal appeals court, and tribal court cases may also reach the Supreme Court through the appeals process.

With original jurisdiction, the whole trial process occurs, including arguments, witnesses, evidence, and so on. Once a case is appealed, the appeals court decides whether justice was done properly in the courts with original jurisdiction. There’s a common misconception that with an appeal, the whole case is heard again, but that isn’t true.

The Supreme Court’s original jurisdiction includes legal disputes involving foreign diplomats or two or more states. However, the Supreme Court does its most important work as an appellate court. Its appellate jurisdiction extends to cases arising under the Constitution, federal law, regulations, and treaties.
