Supreme Court Opinions

The Supreme Court can handle only so many cases in a year, so the cases selected tend to focus on important constitutional issues or issues with major outstanding conflicting precedents. In most cases appealed to the Supreme Court, the court won’t hear the case and will let the lower court ruling stand. When the Supreme Court does hear a case, they’ll make a decision. With this decision will come written opinions, where the justices explain their reasoning for their collective decision. These opinions have become important for legal scholars and lawyers arguing active cases to study, because they explain the precedents set for the nation.

There are four types of US Supreme Court opinions: majority, plurality, concurring, and dissenting.

Select each item to learn more.

Majority Opinion

A majority opinion is a written opinion on behalf of the side that makes up the majority of the Court’s justices, stating the reasoning underlying its decision on a case.

Plurality Opinion

A plurality opinion is a written opinion that, in the absence of a majority opinion, presents the reasoning of most of the justices who decide in favor of the winning party.

Concurring Opinion

A concurring opinion is a written opinion of one or more justices who voted with the majority position but disagree with the majority's reasoning on a case.

Dissenting Opinion

A minority or dissenting opinion is written to explain the reasoning of the justices who ended up having fewer votes on a legal decision before the Supreme Court.
