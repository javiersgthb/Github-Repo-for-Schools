Organization of the Federal Courts

Keep in mind that, due to federalism, there are systems of state courts and a federal system of courts that run parallel to one another. For most crimes and disputes that happen within a state’s borders, the state courts handle those issues. For federal issues, cases follow a specific flow.

District Courts Courts of Appeals The Supreme Court

District courts are courts of original jurisdiction in federal cases. Trials are held to decide the facts of cases. Each state has at least one district court, and larger states may have more. These courts handle a vast majority of the federal case load.

The courts of appeals are courts that handle appeals from the federal district courts. There are 12 courts of appeals in the United States. Lawyers for the defendant and plaintiff (or prosecution) make an argument to a panel of three judges. The judges decide to affirm or reverse the earlier decision. These courts are also known as the circuit courts of appeals because years ago, these courts (and their judges) would travel from location to location in a circuit to handle the judicial business needed in their territory.

The Supreme Court is the highest court in the court system, and serves as the final court of appeals for both the state and federal system. The Supreme Court does have original jurisdiction over a few special kinds of cases, such as those involving representatives of foreign nations and disputes between states.
