Judicial Review

Early on in the Supreme Court’s history, it claimed one of its most fundamental powers—judicial review. This is the power of a court to decide if a new action taken by the executive branch or a new law conflicts with the existing law of the Constitution. If there’s a conflict, the Court can strike down the new action or law. This power wasn’t written into the Constitution for the judicial branch. It was claimed as part of the landmark Supreme Court case, Marbury v. Madison. Judicial review remains in use today when the Supreme Court considers the constitutionality of laws.

Numerous times, the judicial branch has used judicial review to uphold its interpretation of the Constitution and to act as a check on the power of the other branches and levels of government. 

Two examples of judicial review are Roe v. Wade and Citizens United v. The Federal Election Commission.

Roe v. Wade

A famous (if controversial) example of judicial review is the 1973 Roe v. Wade decision. In this case, state laws outlawing abortion were overturned under the right to privacy as interpreted from the Fourteenth Amendment by the Supreme Court of the United States. In this case, state laws were overturned.
