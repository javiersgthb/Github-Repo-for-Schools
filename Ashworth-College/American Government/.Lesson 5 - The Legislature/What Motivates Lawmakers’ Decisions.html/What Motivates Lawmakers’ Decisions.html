What Motivates Lawmakers’ Decisions?

Lawmakers in Congress are frequently pulled in different directions when it comes to their lawmaking and decision making. First and foremost, for any politician, the goal is to get elected and stay elected. With this in mind, many of the decisions made by elected officials make perfect sense. Sometimes they change their minds on important topics, change parties, or seem to act in contradictory ways. These moves can typically be seen through the lens of winning elections. Are there politicians who put their career aside for the greater good? Of course, but unfortunately such a person is sometimes replaced by another official with more political savvy.

Local versus National Needs

The members of Congress are in an interesting position—they need to make policy for the country, but are beholden to the voters in their congressional district or state (for senators). The political reality is that the constituents of an elected US official are the only people that can vote for or against that official. The congressperson must keep the voters at home happy while conducting the nation’s business. This can cause conflicts if what’s good for the people at home isn’t necessarily the best for the nation. For example, an expensive public works project would be popular in a home congressional district, but costly for a national government in debt.

Demands of the Political Party

Congressmen and -women also come under pressure from their political parties. The parties typically help officials get elected, which is their number one goal, and expect loyalty in return. This can cause conflict if what the party wants is different from what constituents want. For example, there would be a conflict if a Democratic congressman from a rural part of the country were asked to support the party stance on further gun ownership restrictions.

Demands of Interest Groups

In the United States, groups of people who work together to achieve their goals in government are known as interest groups. These groups work toward policy that would be beneficial to their group in the lawmaking process. These groups also provide quite a bit of campaign financing to help elected officials win and stay in office. Sometimes, what the interest groups want out of an official can conflict with the demands of the political party or the voters in a congressional district.
