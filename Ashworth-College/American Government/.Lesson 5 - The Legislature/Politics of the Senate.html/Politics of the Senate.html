Politics of the Senate

Because the district lines are mainly drawn by state legislatures controlled by one party or another, politics enters into the equation. Most incumbent representatives and senators who run for reelection are successful. Those elected to Congress aren’t typical American citizens. They’re older and wealthier than most Americans, disproportionately white and male, and more likely to be lawyers. Members of Congress are well-paid and enjoy benefits such as free postage. Members have personal and committee staff and enjoy a number of legal privileges and immunities. Most Congressional representatives (both House and Senate) leave as multimillionaires.

One issue that the United States might face in the future is the naturally unrepresentative nature of the Senate. All states, regardless of population, have two senators. This means that Texas, with a population of approximately 29 million, has the same power in the Senate as Rhode Island, which has a population of 1.05 million.

What’s the biggest disadvantage of the unrepresentative nature of the Senate?

Remember that for a bill to become law, the exact same version of the bill must pass both houses of Congress. Because of the makeup of the Senate, senators representing a minority of Americans (and potentially a very small minority) can shut down all legislation.


The Founding Fathers wanted states, not necessarily people, to have representation. While this may be the case, it’s arguable whether this is a good situation going forward. As more and more Americans become concentrated in city areas or certain states, this imbalance may increase.

Conversely, the Senate is the more stable body of the two houses due to its longer terms of six years rather than two-year terms and less frequent turnover.  Thus, the two chambers served different purposes. The House of Representatives responds to the general population, and the Senate serves the needs of the individual states.
