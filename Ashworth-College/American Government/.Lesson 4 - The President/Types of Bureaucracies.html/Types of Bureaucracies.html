Types of Bureaucracies

All of the federal bureaucracies have been created by acts of Congress, but the executive branch has the responsibility of administering them.

Parts of the federal bureaucracy are organized into four types, depending on the purpose or mission of the federal agency:

  1. Cabinet departments 
  2. Independent executive agencies
  3. Independent regulatory agencies
  4. Government corporations

Cabinet Departments

The president appoints the leaders of the 15 cabinet departments with the approval of the Senate, and these leaders are also part of the line of succession to the presidency. These departments regulate and enforce the laws related to their function. Each department is a vast organization with many smaller groups having specialized roles.

Independent Executive Agencies

Independent executive agencies perform a service rather than regulate a function of government. The president may appoint the leaders. However, these agencies are independent of any executive department and perform a focused goal. For example, the National Aeronautical Space Administration (NASA) is an independent agency dedicated to space exploration.

Independent Regulatory Agencies

Independent regulatory agencies make and enforce rules on matters related to economic activities to serve the public interest. These agencies are generally led by a commission, a panel of officials appointed by the president with the approval of the Senate. Congress made them independent, so they were not influenced by political pressure from either the president or Congress. For example, the Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC) is responsible for regulating the stock market. It was formed after the stock market crash of 1929 to protect investors and stop the manipulation of the stock market.

Government Corporations

Government corporations are government-run businesses that perform a service that likely would not be available without the government's involvement. Leadership is often in the form of a commission with its members appointed by the president with the approval of the Senate. These businesses charge a fee for their services and are supposed to generate enough profit to sustain themselves rather than use public taxes for funding. For example, the Tennessee Valley Authority (TVA) was created to bring electricity to that region of the country, modernize the area, and stimulate its economy during the Great Depression.
