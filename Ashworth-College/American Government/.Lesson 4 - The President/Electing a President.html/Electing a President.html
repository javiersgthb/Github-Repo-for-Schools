Electing a President

Major parties will run several candidates, and the primary election is the candidate’s party election. Representatives are elected to go to the party convention, which is usually in July. These candidates are committed to vote for the party-supported candidate. In the olden days, they could vote for whoever they wanted after the first ballot; however, each party has its own sets of rules on voting at the convention.

In the past, the convention process was a real election, but now with all the rules, it’s known who will win the nomination even before the party’s convention. Usually, the final (symbolic) vote is on Thursday night, but the conventions now are merely pep rallies where candidates kick off their campaigns for the general election in November.

The electoral college is simply a confirmation process. Each state has the same number of electors as it has members in Congress.

If a candidate wins the electoral count for a state, they win that state's vote. If a candidate wins enough states, they win the election.

The electoral college works in a certain way:

  • Before the presidential election, each political party draws up a list of electors who promise to vote for the party’s presidential candidate.

  • On Election Day, when you vote for a certain candidate, you’re really voting for that candidate’s team of electors.

  • A few weeks after the election, the official voting takes place in each state.

  • The votes are then counted in Congress.

  • To win, a candidate needs an absolute majority of electoral votes, 270 or more (out of 538).
