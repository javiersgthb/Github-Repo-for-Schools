Criticisms of the Electoral College

There are criticisms of the electoral college. Two of the main criticisms are the advantage to low population states and the winner-take all system.

Uneven Voting Power Winner-Take-All System

Every state gets a minimum of one elector per representative and a minimum of two electors for each senate seat. (Remember that each state, regardless of size, gets two senators.) This means each state gets a minimum of three electoral votes. While this may seem to be a fair situation on the surface, applying the math proves that low-population states actually have more electoral college power per vote than high population states. This violates the common conception of “one man, one vote” by giving voters in certain states more power than others.

A second criticism is the idea that in most states there’s a winner-take-all system. The only exceptions to this are Nebraska and Maine, each of which split electoral votes by Congressional district. In such a system, the winner of the largest share of popular votes gets all of the electoral votes for that state. In theory, a candidate can win a state by a single vote and get all of the electoral votes for that state. This creates a few problems. First, there could be a significant percentage of voters who don’t have their preference reflected in the electoral vote total. Second, this can lead to candidates simply trying to win a plurality of votes in a state rather than appealing to the widest array of voters. This winner-take-all nature could be changed by the states themselves, as the Constitution allows states to decide how to allocate electoral votes after a presidential election.

These issues have led to continuous arguments for abolishing the electoral college. The loser of the popular vote has still won the presidency twice since 2000—in 2000 and 2016.
